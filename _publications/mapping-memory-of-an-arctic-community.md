The main settlement of the High Arctic archipelago of Svalbard, Longyearbyen, is home to about 2,500 residents. People arrive, stay a few years, and leave. Norwegians stay about six years on average, residents of other nationalities around four. The latter make up more than a third of the town and come from over 50 countries. When people leave, they take with them memories of why things are as they are. What endures is mostly the Svalbard shown to outsiders—through museums, research, and tourism—while the community’s everyday memory slips away.

<!--more-->

A local newspaper like *Svalbardposten* cannot hold memories, but it keeps a continuous record of what a community has argued about and cared for. Our visualization turns this record into a synoptic map of a public conversation in constant reconfiguration. Drawing on Bruno Latour’s approach to mapping controversies, and on the weather map model that Dario Rodighiero developed with Jean Daniélou (2023), we clustered the newspaper’s articles from the last two decades (more than 16,700 articles, *Svalbardposten* 2006–2024; data in Rosado 2026) by semantic similarity, giving form to a network visualization where patterns of discourse emerge. Each cluster was labeled by a large language model and colored on a temperature scale: red marks current and emerging discourses; blue, those that once dominated and are now cooling. Readers can zoom from distant reading down to individual articles with titles, keywords, and short excerpts in both Norwegian and English.

In August 2026, under unusually clear Arctic skies, we brought the network visualization to Longyearbyen to explore with residents how it might matter to them. At a participatory workshop hosted by Artica Svalbard, a local arts organization, attendees gathered in small groups around large prints of the map, exploring their own town’s public conversation. One short-term resident said, “It’s nice for me to see what the conversation was like in 2006.” Another described the map as offering a sense of continuity, something rare in a community always on the move.

That continuity has few other keepers. Longyearbyen’s museums present curated, necessarily selective versions of the past, partly shaped for a tourist gaze: wild nature, expeditions, coal mining. They do address some of the town’s present challenges, but rarely the everyday local debates residents have argued over recently. The map, by contrast, is meant for the people who live there: a shared record of local debates they can browse, discuss, and return to.

Yet on a global scale, this small town sits at the center of competing geopolitical interests. Svalbard belongs to Norway but is governed under the 1920 Svalbard Treaty, which grants citizens of all signatory states equal rights to economic activity. Norway and Russia have long asserted their presence here, first mainly through coal and increasingly through research and tourism. As Svalbard warms faster than almost anywhere on Earth and melting sea ice opens new shipping routes, its strategic weight keeps growing.

On a local scale, residents told us how decisions made in Oslo often overlook local needs. A 2022 rule requiring three years in a mainland Norwegian municipality has kept many foreign residents out of local elections. This leaves local representatives in a difficult position: they must explain a fluid, diverse community to a distant state. That is where workshop participants saw a role for the map. It offers evidence of what residents have actually discussed, which issues keep returning, and for how long. It lets the community be understood as it is, not only through the face it presents to visitors.

Like an actual weather map describing the current atmospheric situation, this visual method cannot tell us what comes next but shows which fronts are forming and which are fading. In a region of constant arrivals and departures, this visualization turns a newspaper’s archive into one of the few places where conversations stay and where newcomers can pick up the thread.
